Marie Skłodowska-Curie (7 November 1867 – 4 July 1934) was a Polish-born French physicist and chemist who pioneered the field of radioactivity, a term she explicitly coined. She remains one of the most celebrated scientists in history, recognized as the first woman to win a Nobel Prize, the only person to win Nobel Prizes in two different scientific fields (Physics and Chemistry), and the first female professor at the University of Paris (Sorbonne). Her groundbreaking discoveries revolutionized the understanding of matter and energy while laying the foundation for modern nuclear physics and cancer treatments.


She:
  • Discovered two new radioactive elements from a mineral uranium ore called pitchblende: polonium (named after her native Poland) and radium.
  • Successfully produced radium as a pure metal in 1910, proving its independent existence to the scientific community beyond doubt.
